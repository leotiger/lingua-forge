# Everybody in This Room Is Wearing a Uniform

*On submitting a plugin, receiving an AI-written name-change request, and what happens when you try to have a real conversation with a bureaucracy.*

---

We submitted Lingua Forge for review a few days ago. For context: the WordPress.org plugin directory is the main distribution channel for free plugins, and getting listed there requires passing a human review. The queue, depending on the period, can stretch for weeks.

What happened next was unexpected. The plugin was pulled from the queue and moved to the front for immediate review. This does not happen routinely. It suggested that something in the submission had caught someone's attention — the name, the description, the scope of the feature set, something. We are choosing to read that as a compliment, at least for now.

---

Then the email arrived.

The review team asked us to change the plugin name. The email was, to a significant extent, written by AI. We cannot say with certainty whether a human reviewer picked a template and filled in the blanks, or whether an AI assistant drafted the message directly — our guess is the former — but the texture of it was unmistakable to anyone who has spent time working alongside these tools. The phrasing was smooth, slightly generic, and had that particular quality of appearing authoritative while making no actual argument.

Before submitting, we had done the work. We checked the WordPress documentation, searched for trademark conflicts, and scanned the wordpress.org namespace carefully. The name "Lingua Forge" had no conflicts with any existing plugin, no trademark entanglement, nothing that should give a careful reader pause. We laid all of this out in our reply, systematically, with references.

We did not receive a response to our arguments. We received the same request again.

---

This is a specific kind of frustration — not the frustration of being told no, but the frustration of a process that has no mechanism for engaging with a *why*. A well-functioning review can reject something and explain the reasoning in terms that connect to documented criteria. A badly-functioning one issues the same instruction twice without addressing the substance of the objection. The second kind is not made more authoritative by its repetition. It is just less useful to talk to.

We raised this — as structural criticism, not complaint — with Matt Mullenweg and with the plugin review team. Among other things, we pointed out an asymmetry that seems worth discussing openly: plugin authors operate under extensive, documented guidelines. What you can name your plugin. What your readme must contain. What your code is not allowed to do. Page after page of it. We asked whether equivalent guidelines existed for reviewers — whether there was any documented basis for what arguments a reviewer had to engage with, what consistency they were expected to maintain across cases, whether a plugin author could request a second opinion when a decision appeared to rest on preference rather than policy.

We also recommended something specific: that it should be possible, from the outside, to see whether the same reviewer is handling a submission throughout its review, or whether reviewers are rotating. Accountability requires traceability. Right now that information is not visible to submitters. This could be achieved without disclosing the identity of the reviewers on the outside...

One reviewer stated, without apparent discomfort, that there are no guidelines for reviewers.

---

What we encountered instead of guidelines was the volunteer argument.

The message, stated or implied, was roughly this: these are unpaid volunteers giving their time, and your expectations should be calibrated accordingly. The subtext was hard to miss. Criticism of the process reads as ingratitude, and ingratitude toward volunteers is bad form. Drop it.

Frank Zappa, addressing a heckler during a concert who had appointed himself the authentic voice against the conformists in the room, said: *Everybody in this room is wearing a uniform, so don't kid yourself...* ¹ Yes, I'm wearing a uniform, everybody does, mine is worn-off a bit and probably does not sit well, the uniform of the established inside the wordpress community seems more polished and uniformed... So ironically the Zappa citation works both ways... right now we are stuck, telling us things about our dress code without addressing the real problem which isn't any other than: Have we passed behind the blocking situation of the plugin?

---
¹ Frank Zappa & the Mothers of Invention, "The Little House I Used to Live In," *Burnt Weeny Sandwich*, Bizarre Records, 1970.

---

Let's dig deeper into what's my interpretation of the Zappa statement: He meant that the act of declaring yourself outside all categories is itself a category. The person performing radical non-conformity is wearing a uniform — it's just sewn differently. The point is not that everyone is a hypocrite. The point is that positioning yourself as exempt from a structure you are in fact operating within is a rhetorical move, not an observation.

The volunteer who positions their role as beyond criticism is doing something structurally similar. No money changing hands does not dissolve the power dynamic. It does not remove the asymmetry between someone who can block a submission with a word and someone who has no clear recourse when that decision goes unexplained. The uniform is still there. It just doesn't come with a salary.

We are also, by any reasonable definition, a volunteer. We built a plugin, made it free, and submitted it to a directory so that others could use it without paying for something that should arguably exist in WordPress core. That is not a claim to special treatment — it is a reminder that the volunteer framing can be deployed selectively, and the moment it gets used to forestall criticism rather than describe a situation, it has changed its function. It has become a defence. And it is not a particularly strong one.

---

On the plugin review Slack, the advice for cases like ours was practical: ask a direct question. Reviewers respond to direct questions. It was said as if it were reliable.

We asked twice. Politely, specifically — did we address the issue? Is the blocking concern resolved? — following the recommendation to the letter. No response.

There is a quality to institutional silence that is different from both yes and no. It carries none of the information of a decision and all of the practical effect of one. At a certain point, and at my age this clarity arrives more quickly than it used to: Is it worth it? Do I need this community? The older you get the more complicated it gets to adapt to group dynamics and group dynamics tend to "recompense" obedience in the beginning... I can perfectly live without what seems a bit of "kindergarten" from the perspective of an old and outgrown guy. If the plugin isn't on wordpress.org, why not, I can still distribute it, with less audience and less users... won't be the first that's useful and not on workpress.org and it means less responsibility and less workload...

---

Schumpeter wrote — he was thinking about capitalism when he did, but the observation travels — that decline begins when an organisation loses its capacity for self-renewal. Not from external pressure. From within: when administration starts to crowd out creation, when in-groups form around the authority to say no, when the purpose the institution was built to serve gets quietly displaced by the purpose of maintaining the institution itself. When the organisation's self-awareness goes, its vitality tends to follow.

The WordPress plugin directory was built to connect useful software with the people who need it. That is the purpose. Whether the review process currently serves that purpose, or whether it has developed interests of its own that sit somewhat apart from it, is a reasonable question to ask — not to attack people who give their time, but because institutions that take that question seriously tend to stay useful for longer than institutions that don't.

We are still here. The plugin is still free. We still believe the ecosystem is worth building for.

We just think someone should say this clearly, once, without the uniform argument making it impolite to do so. The author is wearing a uniform himself, it's simply different...
