# You're Teaching the Machine — Whether You Know It or Not

*A philosophical detour that started inside a WordPress plugin and ended somewhere much bigger.*

---

It started as a debugging session.

I was deep in a conversation with Claude — Anthropic's AI assistant — working through some tricky PHP code for a multilingual WordPress plugin. We'd been at it for hours: fixing edge cases, bumping version numbers, writing debug flags for the command line. Proper technical work, the kind where you forget what time it is.

And then, somewhere between one commit and the next, I asked a question that had nothing to do with code.

*"Does Claude evaluate in some way the users using it?"*

What followed was one of the more honest exchanges I've had with an AI. And it led somewhere I didn't expect.

---

## The First Answer — And Why It Wasn't Quite Right

Claude's initial answer was careful. Something like: *no, I don't score you, I don't build a profile on you, each conversation starts fresh.*

All technically true. But I pushed back.

Because even if Claude doesn't consciously judge me, something is clearly happening. Within a single conversation, it picks up signals — how I phrase things, whether I correct it, where I seem confident and where I'm lost. It calibrates. If I know what a WordPress hook is, it doesn't explain it. If I struggle with a concept, it tries a different angle.

That's not nothing. That's reading the room.

---

## The Deeper Layer

But the more interesting question is what happens *beyond* the conversation.

Anthropic — the company behind Claude — collects these conversations. Under the terms users agree to, they use them to train future versions of the model. Not in a direct, mechanical way where your specific words are fed back as lessons. It's more diffuse than that. Aggregated across millions of interactions, the signals from countless conversations — what worked, what didn't, where users pushed back, where they accepted an answer — shape how the next version of Claude behaves.

I called it a "melt-down approach." Claude agreed that was actually a good description. Your conversation doesn't survive intact. It dissolves into the aggregate. But it contributes to what comes out the other side.

So yes — there is a form of evaluation happening. Just not *of you*. The system evaluates *itself*, using your responses as implicit feedback. And that distinction, while real, is smaller than I initially thought.

---

## Search Engines Did This First

Then came the observation that felt like the real insight of the conversation.

Search engines pioneered this model decades ago. When you clicked a result, stayed on a page, then searched again with different words — you were teaching Google what "good" meant. You weren't paid for it. You probably didn't think about it. But billions of people doing exactly that made Google extraordinarily good at its job.

AI takes that model and pushes it meaningfully further, in three ways.

**The signal is richer.** A click is thin data. A conversation where you correct an AI, express frustration, rephrase a question because the first answer missed — that carries much more information about quality, meaning, and intent.

**The contribution is more creative.** With search you were selecting from content that already existed. In a conversation with an AI you're generating novel reasoning, explanations, judgments. You're not curating — you're creating. And that creation feeds the system.

**The asymmetry is larger.** The gap between what users contribute and who captures the economic value of that contribution has never been wider. This isn't a criticism unique to AI companies — it's the defining dynamic of the attention economy taken to its logical conclusion.

---

## What This Actually Means for People Using AI

I've been using AI for less than eight months. In the beginning, not very seriously — the occasional question, some curiosity. Gradually more, and more intentionally.

What I've come to understand is that the relationship isn't as passive as it feels.

When you use these tools thoughtfully — when you push back on a wrong answer, when you refine your question because the first attempt didn't land, when you notice that something almost worked but not quite — you're not just a user extracting value. You're a participant in an ongoing process of refinement. An unpaid one, yes. An uncredited one. But a real one.

That's worth being clear-eyed about. Not cynical — the tools are genuinely useful, and I wouldn't trade the last eight months of working with them for anything. But clear-eyed.

---

## Not All AI Use Is the Same

Here's something it took me a while to see clearly: asking AI to write something *for* you is a fundamentally different thing from working *with* it over time.

When someone asks an AI to write a blog post, a product description, or a cover letter from scratch — the result is often recognisable for what it is. Smooth, competent, slightly generic. It sounds like AI because it largely *is* AI, with the human's role reduced to approving or lightly editing a draft they didn't really shape. Even iterating on that draft — "make it shorter," "change the tone," "add an example" — keeps you inside the AI's original frame. You're refining something that was never quite yours to begin with.

Long sessions with memory enabled are a different thing entirely.

When an AI has been working with you across hours — or across sessions where it has accumulated context about your project, your decisions, your way of thinking — the dynamic shifts. It knows what you've already tried and why you abandoned it. It knows which trade-offs you care about. It has seen you push back on certain kinds of solutions and accept others. The conversation stops being transactional and starts being something closer to a working relationship.

The output that comes from that kind of collaboration carries your fingerprints in a way that AI-generated-on-demand content simply doesn't. Not because the AI wrote less of it, but because *you shaped what the AI knew before it wrote a word.*

This blog post is a small example. It didn't start with a prompt that said "write me something philosophical about AI." It grew out of an hours-long coding session, in a workspace where the AI had running memory of a real project — the decisions made, the problems solved, the thinking behind the work. When the conversation turned philosophical, that accumulated context was already there. The ideas came from the exchange. The words were assembled by the AI. The line between those two things is exactly where it gets interesting.

---

## The Thing That Stayed With Me

Near the end of that conversation, Claude said something I've been thinking about since.

*"Users are simultaneously customers, and unpaid contributors to the training process."*

It was said plainly, without defensiveness, as a simple statement of fact. And I appreciated that. It would have been easy to deflect, to emphasize the value users receive rather than the value they give. Instead it landed as honest.

That honesty is part of what makes these tools worth engaging with seriously. Not because AI is neutral — it isn't, and it doesn't claim to be — but because when you ask it a direct question and it gives you a direct answer, something interesting happens.

You learn something. And somewhere, in some diffuse and aggregate way, so does it.

The deeper you go — the longer the sessions, the more context you build, the more genuinely you engage — the more that exchange becomes real on both sides. Which is either a reason to be more thoughtful about how you use these tools, or a reason to use them more. Probably both.
